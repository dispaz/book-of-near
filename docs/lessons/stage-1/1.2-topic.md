# Topic 1.2: Access keys (full-access and function-call)

The source of truth for lesson 1.2. The lesson script (`1.2.md`) and the code may only teach what this
file says. Facts carry a source tag; anything else is marked (unverified).

## In one sentence

A NEAR account can hold many keys, and each key has its own power: a **full-access key** can do
anything, a **function-call key** can only call one contract and spend a little NEAR on gas.

## The problem it solves

On most chains an account is one key pair. Whoever holds the private key can do everything: send all
the money, sign anything. So you can never let an app or a helper sign for you without trusting it
with everything. [eth-accounts]

NEAR splits "the account" from "the key". The account is a name (lesson 1.1). Keys are attached to
it, and you can attach more than one. [near-keys] That lets you hand out a key that is useful but
cannot hurt you much.

## How it works on NEAR

**Many keys per account.** "In NEAR, accounts can hold multiple keys." No maximum is stated. [near-keys]

**Full-access key.** Can do anything on the account: send NEAR, deploy code, call contracts, and add
or remove keys. The key you got in lesson 1.1 is one. [near-keys]

**Function-call key.** A key with three settings, all checked by the chain on every transaction:
[near-keys]

| Setting | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `receiver_id` | The one contract this key may call. No other contract. |
| `method_names` (optional) | Which methods of that contract. Empty means all of them. |
| `allowance` (optional) | The most NEAR this key may spend on gas. Empty means unlimited. |

What it can never do: send NEAR, attach NEAR to a call, add or remove keys, delete the account.
[near-keys]

**Who it is for.** The docs: function-call keys "are meant to be shared with applications, so
third-parties can make contract calls in your name." [near-keys] A game or app holds such a key, so it
can make its own calls (move a piece, post a message) without asking you to approve each one.
(How a specific wallet creates this key at "log in" is unverified; don't describe a login flow until it
is checked.)

**The allowance.** It belongs to the key, not to the account balance. When it is used up, the key is
unusable; adding NEAR to the account does not revive it. An allowance cannot be changed: add a new key
and delete the old. [near-keys]

**Taking a key back.** Delete it with a full-access key. After that the chain refuses anything it
signs. [near-keys]

## Words to know

- **Key pair:** a private key that signs and a public key anyone can check (lesson 0.2).
- **Access key:** a public key attached to a NEAR account, with a permission.
- **Full-access key:** an access key that can do anything on its account.
- **Function-call key:** an access key limited to one contract, optional method names and a gas allowance.
- **Allowance:** the most NEAR a function-call key may spend on gas.
- **Contract:** code that lives on an account. Its methods are what a function-call key may call.
- **Revoke:** delete a key so it can no longer sign.

## Common misunderstandings

- **"A limited key is safe, full stop."** It is limited, not harmless. It can still call any allowed
  method of its contract until the allowance runs out. A bad method on that contract is still bad. [near-keys]
- **"The allowance is money the key can spend."** Only on gas. It can never move NEAR. [near-keys]
- **"Giving the key to an app is the same as connecting a wallet."** Connecting can mean many things;
  the key is the on-chain fact. What it can do is written on the chain and readable by anyone. [near-keys]
- **"I can ask for the key back."** A key stays valid until it is deleted on chain.
- **"This is an AI feature."** It is not. The chain does not know or care who holds a key: a person, an
  app, a server or an AI agent. It only checks the permission.

## Compared with Ethereum

A plain Ethereum account (an externally-owned account) is one key pair; the private key controls
everything. [eth-accounts] Limits like "this helper may only do X" need a smart-contract wallet
(account abstraction), which is an add-on, not part of the plain account. [eth-aa]

## Strengths and weaknesses (feeds the Honest take)

Strengths: limits live in the account and are checked by the chain; a leaked limited key has a
ceiling; revoking is one transaction. [near-keys]

Weaknesses: limits are coarse (one contract, method names, gas only; no "1 NEAR per day"); allowance
can't be changed; a limited key can still misuse its own contract. [near-keys]

## What the lesson must show

1. An account with one full-access key, and why handing that key out is too much.
2. A function-call key being added, with its three settings read back from the chain.
3. The key doing the one thing it is for, and the allowance going down.
4. The chain refusing what the key may not do (send NEAR, call another contract, add a key).
5. Deleting the key, and the chain refusing it afterwards.
