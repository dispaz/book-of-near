# Lesson 1.2: A key that can do only one thing

Issue: #15. Target time: 6 minutes. Chain access: testnet (real transactions from the practice account) or demo mode (DemoChain, same rules). Everything goes through the `Chain` interface (`docs/chain.md`). The key holder is an app (the Counter app), played by the lesson itself.

Topic: `1.2-topic.md` (the source of truth for what this lesson teaches).

Rewrite (7 Nov 2026): the first version framed the key holder as an "AI agent" with a prompt box,
although no model is called. Learners read it as a lesson about AI and missed the point: **an account
can hold many keys, with different powers**. This version uses the case the docs give: a key shared
with an **app**, so it can make contract calls in your name [near-keys]. Agents appear only as one
example in the Honest take. The chain mechanics are unchanged (counter contract, three refusals,
delete), so the code changes are in copy and controls, not in the Chain interface.

## 1. Learning goals

1. Say that a NEAR account can hold several keys, and tell a full-access key from a function-call key.
2. Give an app a function-call key limited to one contract and a gas allowance, and read it back from the chain.
3. See the chain refuse that key when it tries to send NEAR, call another contract or add a key.
4. Delete the key and see the chain refuse it afterwards.

## 2. Steps

### Step 1: Hook

- **Caption:** A small app wants to save your clicks on the chain. Would you give it the key to your whole account?
- **Explain:** Your practice account has one key, a **full-access key**. Whoever holds it can do anything: send all your NEAR, add keys, delete the account. An app that only counts clicks needs far less. On NEAR an account can hold **many keys**, and a key can be limited.
- **Scene:** The account (mailbox) with its one key on a ring, and the Counter app (a small contract machine) next to it with an empty key hook. No gate.

### Step 2: Two kinds of key

- **Caption:** A full-access key can do anything. A function-call key can call one contract and spend a little on gas. Nothing else.
- **Explain:** A **function-call key** names one contract, and optionally which of its methods. Its **allowance** is the most NEAR it may spend on **gas** (the fee for the work a transaction causes). It can never send NEAR, attach NEAR to a call or add keys. The chain checks this on every transaction.
- **Scene:** The two keys side by side. The full-access key is plain; the function-call key has a tag listing contract, methods and allowance, with "can / cannot" beside it. No gate.

### Step 3: Give the app a key

- **Caption:** Set an allowance, then give the Counter app its own key. It will be able to call only the counter.
- **Explain:** One transaction does two things: it puts a small **counter contract** on your account (the app's code), and it adds a new function-call key that may call only that contract. The app keeps the private half of the new key. The app then reads the key back from the chain, so what you see is what the chain holds. Your account now has **two keys**.
- **Action:** Field: allowance in NEAR (0.01 to 0.5, default 0.1). Button **Give the app a key**. After it runs, the scene shows the account's key list with both keys, and the new key's tag: contract, methods ("any method of this contract"), allowance. Gate opens when the key reads back.

### Step 4: The app does its job

- **Caption:** Click Count. The app signs with its own key, and you are not asked to approve. Watch the {allowance} NEAR allowance drop.
- **Explain:** The app calls `increment` on the counter, signed with its function-call key, not yours. The chain checks: right contract, enough allowance. It accepts. The gas comes out of the key's allowance, not your balance.
- **Action:** Button **Count** (can be clicked more than once). The scene shows an envelope go from the app to the counter, the counter go up and the allowance gauge fall. Gate opens after one success.

### Step 5: What if the app goes bad?

- **Caption:** Suppose the app is hacked. The attacker has its key. Try three things it should not be able to do.
- **Explain:** The attacker can sign anything with the app's key. The chain does not trust the app; it checks each transaction against the key's permission. A refused transaction is not included and costs nothing.
- **Action:** Three buttons, each signed with the app's key: **Send NEAR to the attacker** (a transfer), **Call another contract** (the `testnet` registrar), **Add a full-access key for the attacker**. Each refusal shows the chain's message and reason in an ink-red box, and the envelope bounces back with a red cross. Gate opens after all three are refused.

### Step 6: Take the key back

- **Caption:** You are done with the app. Delete its key. It still had {left} NEAR of its {allowance} NEAR allowance.
- **Explain:** To take a key back you delete it, with your full-access key. Asking the app to forget it changes nothing on the chain. Whatever allowance was left goes with it.
- **Action:** Button **Delete the app's key**. The scene strikes the key out and the account's key list shows one key again. Then **Count** is offered once more; the chain refuses it because the key no longer exists. Gate opens when the key is gone.

### Step 7: Quick check (summary page)

- **Caption:** Two quick questions. Then the honest take.
- **Action:** Check (section 4). Completion record notes the refused attempts and the delete transaction as evidence.

## 3. Glossary terms introduced

- **Full-access key:** a key that can do anything on its account.
- **Function-call key:** a key limited to calling one contract (optionally some of its methods), with a gas allowance.
- **Allowance:** the most NEAR a function-call key may spend on gas.
- **Gas:** from 0.3: a measure of the work a transaction causes.
- **Contract:** code that lives on an account. Here a tiny counter.
- **Revoke:** take a key's power away by deleting it.

## 4. Check

**Q1. What can a function-call key not do?**
- A. Send NEAR to another account. **(correct)**
- B. Call a method on the one contract it names. *Wrong: that is the one thing it is for.*
- C. Pay gas from its allowance. *Wrong: gas is exactly what the allowance covers.*

**Q2. How do you take back a key you gave to an app?**
- A. Delete the key with a transaction signed by your full-access key. **(correct)**
- B. Ask the app to forget it. *Wrong: a key stays valid on the chain until it is deleted.*
- C. Move all your NEAR to another account. *Wrong: the allowance belongs to the key, not to your balance, and the key keeps working.*

## 5. Honest take

Comparison: Ethereum.

**Strengths**
- **Limits live in the account, not in an app.** A NEAR account can hold many keys, and a function-call key is limited to one contract, optional method names and a gas allowance. The chain checks it on every transaction. [near-keys]
- **A leaked limited key has a ceiling.** It cannot move NEAR or attach a deposit, and when its allowance is spent it stops working. [near-keys]
- **Revoking is one transaction.** Delete the key with a full-access key. [near-keys]
- **It works for any helper.** The chain does not know who holds a key: an app, a server, a bot or an AI agent. The same limits apply.

**Weaknesses**
- **The limits are coarse.** A key is bound to one contract and, at most, method names. It cannot say "at most 1 NEAR per day" or "only to this friend". Finer rules need a contract. [near-keys]
- **The allowance is only for gas.** A key can still call a bad method on its one contract, and allowed calls can run until the allowance is gone. [near-keys]
- **An allowance cannot be changed.** To give a different one, add a new key and delete the old. [near-keys]
- **Ethereum can do similar things, with extra setup.** A plain Ethereum account has one private key that signs for everything. Smart-contract wallets can add their own rules, such as trusted addresses, but those are an add-on, not part of the plain account. [eth-accounts] [eth-aa]

## 6. Demo-mode data

- Practice account and 5 NEAR as in 0.3. The contract is the demo counter (`demoContractCode("counter")`), deployed onto the practice account in the same transaction as the key. On testnet the same transaction deploys `contracts/counter/counter.wasm`.
- The app's key is made with `chain.newKeyPair()`; transactions signed by it pass `publicKey` in the request.
- Attacks and the refusals `DemoChain` gives (`docs/chain.md`, Errors): send funds, `RequiresFullAccess`; call `testnet.create_account`, `ReceiverMismatch`; add a full-access key, `RequiresFullAccess`. After the delete, the last Count is refused with `AccessKeyNotFound`. Others the chain knows but the lesson does not offer: `DepositWithFunctionCall`, `MethodNameMismatch`, `NotEnoughAllowance`. These error names are from nearcore (the NEAR node software) as modelled by DemoChain; **confirm each on testnet** before the lesson relies on the exact name. The lesson counts an attempt as refused only when the Chain gives a refusal `reason`.
- Gas for the app's call: 5 Tgas attached. The allowance is charged for the gas held, then credited back after the refund, so a key needs more than the final cost. [near-keys] The price is not set by the docs page: DemoChain holds gas at 0.001 NEAR per Tgas, while testnet's measured gas price is 0.0001 NEAR per Tgas (`docs/spikes-seed.md`, Result log). Numbers on screen come from the chain, not from this note.
- Testnet note (unverified): the network may check the sender's balance before the key's permission, so the "send funds" attack uses an amount the account can afford (balance minus 0.1 NEAR). This matches the note in `docs/chain.md` but is not confirmed for this lesson.
- If a call is included but fails (for example a contract panic), it is shown as "Failed", is not a refusal, and does not count toward the three attempts.
- On load the lesson picks up an existing function-call key on the practice account that targets itself, so a reload does not orphan a key. On testnet the learner may use it only if its secret is still in the browser's key store; otherwise the call errors and the learner deletes the key.
