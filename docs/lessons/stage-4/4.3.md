# Lesson 4.3: Old bridge vs the NEAR way

Issue: #25. Target time: 5 minutes. Chain access: none (interactive comparison matrix and exploit simulation) or demo mode. Views: 2D and 3D, one shared state.

Topic: `4.3-topic.md` (the source of truth for what this lesson teaches).

## 1. Learning goals

1. Compare the multi-step mechanics of classic lock-and-mint bridges with NEAR intent settlement.
2. Understand why multi-million dollar bridge smart contract vaults create catastrophic honeypot risks.
3. Observe how delivering native assets shields users from wrapped token de-pegging exploits.

## 2. Steps

### Step 1: Hook (The \$2.8 Billion bridge problem)

- **Caption:** Over \$2.8 billion has been stolen in cross-chain bridge hacks. Why are bridges so fragile?
- **Explain:** Classic bridges lock massive pools of money in smart contract vaults on one chain and mint synthetic "wrapped tokens" on another. If the vault is hacked, the wrapped tokens become completely worthless IOUs.
- **Scene:** Two parallel tracks side by side: Path A ("The Old Bridge") featuring a massive vault honeypot, and Path B ("The NEAR Way") featuring direct solver settlement. No gate.

### Step 2: Step-by-step path comparison

- **Caption:** Count the steps: 6 manual transactions vs. 1 simple intent declaration.
- **Explain:** The old way requires: Approve token, Deposit in vault, Wait for multi-sig relayers, Mint wrapped token, Swap wrapped token, and later Burn wrapped token. The NEAR way requires: Express intent, Solver delivers native assets.
- **Action:** Toggle comparison criteria "Workflow Steps". Path A lights up with 6 complex steps (~20 mins); Path B lights up with 2 simple steps (~25s). Next is open.

### Step 3: Inspect the wrapped token honeypot

- **Caption:** Examine what a wrapped token actually is: a synthetic IOU, not the real coin.
- **Explain:** If you hold `wETH` or `USDC.e` on an alt-L1, you do not hold real Ethereum or Circle dollars—you hold a claim ticket against a bridge vault contract.
- **Scene:** Path A shows a fragile paper IOU slip tied to the vault. Path B shows a solid native gold coin delivered directly to the learner's mailbox.
- **Action:** Button **Inspect asset backing**. Gate opens immediately.

### Step 4: Simulate a bridge vault hack

- **Caption:** An attacker exploits a vulnerability in the bridge vault contract. Watch what happens!
- **Explain:** A hacker drains the collateral from the Bridge Vault on Ethereum. On Path A, all synthetic wrapped tokens immediately turn red with an ink cross and collapse to zero value. On Path B, the intent learner holds canonical native assets and loses nothing.
- **Action:** Button **Trigger simulated vault exploit**. The Path A vault cracks open in red ink; wrapped token balances shatter. Path B remains completely safe and untouched.
- **Evidence:** `{ exploitSimulated: true, protectedByNativeDelivery: true }`.

### Step 5: Confidential intents overview

- **Caption:** How NEAR protects institutional traders: Confidential Intents hide orders from front-runners.
- **Explain:** On public blockchains, large trades are visible in the mempool, allowing predatory arbitrage bots to front-run orders. NEAR supports **Confidential Intents**, where trade parameters are encrypted in memory until matched.
- **Action:** Button **Inspect confidential shield**. The trade parameters wrap in an encrypted ink shield. Next is open.

### Step 6: Quick check (summary page)

- **Caption:** Three questions on bridge risks, wrapped tokens, and native intent delivery.
- **Action:** Check (section 3).

### Step 7: Honest take

- **Caption:** Honest take: Intent solver spreads vs. bridge vault risks.
- **Gate:** Gated until the exploit simulation in step 4 is completed.

## 3. Check

**Q1. What makes classic lock-and-mint bridges vulnerable to multi-million dollar hacks?**
- A. They store massive pools of user collateral in centralized or multi-sig smart contract vaults. **(correct)**
- B. They run on solar power. *Wrong: blockchain consensus is computational, not dependent on solar panels.*
- C. They charge zero fees to users. *Wrong: bridges charge substantial fees.*

**Q2. If a bridge vault holding real USDC is hacked, what happens to the synthetic wrapped tokens on other chains?**
- A. They become unbacked IOUs and their value collapses towards zero. **(correct)**
- B. The Federal Reserve automatically reimburses all token holders. *Wrong: self-custodial crypto has no FDIC insurance.*
- C. The wrapped tokens turn into real gold. *Wrong: synthetic tokens lose backing when vaults are drained.*

**Q3. How does NEAR's intent model avoid creating bridge honeypots?**
- A. Users receive native assets directly; solvers assume the cross-chain inventory risk themselves. **(correct)**
- B. By making all cross-chain transfers illegal. *Wrong: NEAR enables seamless multichain interaction.*
- C. By deleting all smart contracts from existence. *Wrong: smart contracts are used for verifier settlement.*

## 4. Honest take

**Strengths:**
- **Zero user honeypot exposure:** Users never deposit into shared multi-million-dollar collateral pools that attract hackers. [near-bridge-risk]
- **Native canonical assets:** Users receive genuine tokens (like native USDC) rather than synthetic bridge IOUs. [near-intents]
- **Order confidentiality:** Confidential intents encrypt trade sizes, preventing toxic front-running and MEV. [near-intents]

**Weaknesses:**
- **Solver spread fees:** Market makers charge a fractional spread or fee to compensate for holding inventory capital across multiple chains. [spike-6]
- **Destination chain liquidity:** For rarely traded tokens, users may experience higher spreads if only a few solvers quote that asset. [near-intents]

Compared with: **Traditional Bridges (Wormhole, Multichain, Ronin)** (massive collateral pools, synthetic wrapped token de-peg risks, slow finality).
